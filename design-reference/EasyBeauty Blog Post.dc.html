Niacinamide, without the internet noise

What 4% actually does to a compromised barrier, why the 10% versions sting, and how to layer it with acids without undoing either.

Niacinamide is the most recommended and least understood active in a modern routine. It appears at 2% in barrier creams and at 20% in serums sold on the same claim, which tells you something is off in how the ingredient is being marketed. The dose-response curve flattens far earlier than the packaging suggests.

What it actually does

Vitamin B3 is a precursor to NAD+ and NADP+, two coenzymes involved in the synthesis of ceramides and free fatty acids. When you apply it topically, keratinocytes get the raw material to rebuild the lipid matrix that holds water in the stratum corneum. That is the whole mechanism, and it is a slow one: measurable barrier improvement appears at four weeks, not four days.

Two secondary effects are well documented. It interrupts the transfer of melanosomes from melanocytes to keratinocytes, which is why it reads as brightening on post-inflammatory marks. And it reduces sebum output in oily skin at concentrations above 2%.

Why 10% stings

Two things happen in high-percentage formulas. First, niacinamide is hygroscopic and mildly acidic in solution, so at high load it draws water and shifts the formula pH downward unless it is buffered carefully. Second, and more often the real culprit, commercial niacinamide can carry trace nicotinic acid — a known vasodilator. That is the flushing people describe as a tingle.

Above roughly 5%, you are not buying more benefit. You are buying more chance of irritation.

We formulate at 4% for that reason. It sits above the threshold for sebum regulation and well above the barrier-support dose, and it stays inside the tolerance range for reactive skin.

Layering it with acids

The claim that niacinamide cancels out vitamin C comes from a 1960s study on heated solutions, not on skin. In a finished formula at room temperature, the interaction is negligible. What does matter is pH: exfoliating acids need a low pH to work, and niacinamide nudges it up. Give them room.

One caveat

If your skin is actively compromised — stinging with water, visibly flushed — stop all actives for a week and use only cleanser, a ceramide cream, and sunscreen. Niacinamide supports repair; it does not outrun damage you are still causing.

Where it sits in our range

Two formulas carry it. The serum pairs 4% niacinamide with hyaluronic acid and ceramides for morning use; the SPF fluid carries a lower load so it is not doing the heavy lifting there.
